Before Snakes & Ladders: How Ancient India’s Game of Karma Mapped the Human Soul

Long before it became a nursery pastime in Victorian England or American living rooms, Gyan Chaupar (“The Game of Knowledge”) was a sacred map of the cosmos—where every throw of cowrie shells tested the soul’s balance between virtue and vice on its climb toward liberation.

1. Dice, Destiny & The Medieval Mystics

India’s fascination with dice stretches back over four millennia. Archaeologists unearthing the Indus Valley cities of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro (c. 2500 BCE) found finely carved terracotta and cubical dice alongside cowrie shells (kaudi) and oblong four-sided ivory rods (pasaka). In the Rig-Veda, the famous “Gambler’s Lament” mourns the intoxicating pull of tumbling dice, while the Mahābhārata hinges upon Yudhishthira’s fateful dice match—and Purāṇic lore frequently pictures Lord Shiva and Parvati playing dice across the cosmic ages.

Out of this ancient culture of chance emerged a radical philosophical question: What if a board game could teach the laws of Karma instead of mere fortune? By the 10th century CE, the Jain poet Dhanapāla explicitly referenced a “gyan-chausa” (game of knowledge) in his verse work Rishabhapañcaśikā (c. 972 CE). Three centuries later in Maharashtra, popular tradition credits the 13th-century poet-saint Sant Dnyaneshwar (Gyandev) with refining the 72-square Moksha Patam (“Cloth of Liberation”) so seekers could experience the unseen mechanics of spiritual ascent and karmic downfall with their own hands.

“The oldest surviving painted cloth board dates to 17th-century Mewar in Rajasthan. Often shaped like the silhouette of the Cosmic Being (Purusha), these manuscripts placed the mortal realm at the feet and the gate of liberation (Moksha-dwar) at the crown of the head.”

2. One Board, Four Faiths: Across South Asia

Rather than remaining confined to a single sect, Gyan Chaupar proved remarkably adaptable across India’s spiritual landscape—evolving distinct regional architectures while keeping its core moral engine intact:

Hindu · Gyan Chaupar & Paramapada (72 Squares)

Arranged in an 8×9 Vedic grid ascending through the seven Lokas (planes of existence) to Vaikuntha (Square 68). In Tamil Nadu and Kerala, known as Paramapada Sopana Patam (“Steps to the Highest Place”), families still play the game all night during the sacred vigil of Vaikuntha Ekadashi.

Jain · Gyanbazi (84 Squares)

Cultivated by Jain monks in Gujarat and Rajasthan on a 9×9 grid plus three celestial tiers culminating in Siddhashila (realm of liberated souls). Notably, a late-18th-century Jain board in New Delhi’s National Museum stations a serpent at Square 76 named Mohani-karma (“delusion karma”), warning that spiritual pride can strike at the very threshold of freedom.

Himalayan Pahari · Bifurcated Boards (300+ Squares)

In the Vaishnava hill courts of Punjab and Himachal, artists painted monumental boards exceeding 300 to 380 squares, splitting into left and right cosmic wings before converging on Vishnu’s celestial abode.

Sufi Islamic · Shatranj al-‘Urafa (72–100 Squares)

Medieval Sufi mystics in Mughal and Deccani courts adapted the board into “The Chess of the Gnostics.” Ladders became Islamic mystic stations like Tauba (repentance) and Ishq (divine love), snakes embodied vices like Kibr (arrogance) and Nafs (ego), and the summit represented Fanaa—union with the Divine.

3. The Architecture of Virtue and Vice

Every square on a traditional Gyan Chaupar cloth was inscribed with a psychological state, cosmic plane, or karmic consequence—numbered in a boustrophedon (“ox-turning”) zigzag path from Square 1 (Janma, birth) to the upper realms:

  • Sopāna (The Ladders of Dharma): Ladders were deliberately fewer in number than serpents, teaching that self-realization is a narrow, intentional path. Landing on Bhakti (Devotion, Sq. 17) or Gyan (True Knowledge, Sq. 45) catapults the seeker straight into the celestial planes—sometimes straight to Vaikuntha.
  • Nāga (The Serpents of Adharma): Snakes outnumber ladders by nearly two to one, illustrating how easily the mind slips into Krodha (Wrath), Lobha (Greed), or Ahamkara (Ego). The deadliest serpent on the 72-square Hindu board is Tamogun / Mahāmāyā (Square 66)—placed just two steps away from liberation, plunging the arrogant seeker all the way back to Square 2 (Māyā, Illusion).
  • Karmic Precision at the Summit: Reaching liberation required an exact roll; overshooting the final row meant your soul remained bound to the upper heavens until equilibrium was achieved.

4. From Moksha to the Victorian Parlor

During the 18th and 19th centuries, British East India Company officials and travelers began collecting intricately painted Gyan Chaupar manuscripts (many of which now reside in the Victoria & Albert Museum and the British Library). When the game was introduced to Britain in 1892 (pioneered by game maker F.H. Ayres on a 100-square circular board), its Hindu–Jain metaphysics of reincarnation and cosmic liberation were scrubbed away and replaced with Victorian Sunday-school morality:

Instead of Dharma leading to Moksha, Victorian children climbed ladders labeled Thrift → Fulfillment, Penitence → Grace, and Industry → Success, while sliding down snakes of Indulgence → Disgrace and Disobedience → Sorrow. Notably, the British version equalized the number of ladders and snakes, subtly shifting the game from a perilous spiritual pilgrimage to a tidy bourgeois lesson in civic behavior.

Fifty years later, in 1943, American publisher Milton Bradley adapted the game as Chutes and Ladders—removing snakes altogether (deemed too frightening for young children) in favor of playground slides, and replacing the ancient iconography with cartoon children performing good or naughty deeds.

6. Timeline: 4,500 Years of Sacred Play

c. 2500 BCE — Indus Valley Dice & Shells

Terracotta cubical dice, ivory oblong rods (pasaka), and cowrie shells are used across Harappa and Mohenjo-daro.

c. 1500–500 BCE — Vedic & Epic Literature

The Rig-Veda’s Gambler’s Hymn and the Mahābhārata establish dice casting as a metaphor for cosmic fate and dharma.

972 CE — Dhanapāla’s Rishabhapañcaśikā

Earliest explicit literary reference to a spiritual board game of liberation (gyan-chausa) in medieval Jain poetry.

13th Century CE — Sant Dnyaneshwar in Maharashtra

Popularizes the 72-square Moksha Patam as a devotional teaching aid among Bhakti communities.

17th–18th Centuries CE — Golden Age of Painted Cloth Boards

Oldest extant Mewar board (17th c.) and flourishing of Jain 84-square Gyanbazi, Pahari 300-square boards, and Sufi Shatranj al-‘Urafa manuscripts.

1892 & 1943 — Colonial Export & Western Secularization

F.H. Ayres publishes Snakes and Ladders in Victorian England (1892), followed by Milton Bradley’s Chutes and Ladders in the USA (1943).

21st Century — Cultural & Digital Revival

Museum exhibitions, scholarship, and interactive reconstructions restore the philosophical depth of original Sanskrit and Prakrit boards.

7. Museum Collections & Further Reading

Original painted cloth and paper Gyan Chaupar boards are preserved in the National Museum (New Delhi), the Calico Museum of Textiles (Ahmedabad), Mehrangarh Fort Museum (Jodhpur), the Victoria & Albert Museum and British Library (London), and the Fowler Museum at UCLA.

Key Scholarship: Andrew Topsfield, “The Indian Game of Snakes and Ladders” (Artibus Asiae, 1985) & “Snakes and Ladders in India: Some Further Discoveries” (2006); Deepak Shimkhada, “A Preliminary Study of the Game of Karma in India, Nepal, and Tibet” (1983); Irving Finkel (British Museum board game studies); and contemporary studies by Mohit Srivastava, Elena Vasquez, and Urvashi Chanda-Vaz.
